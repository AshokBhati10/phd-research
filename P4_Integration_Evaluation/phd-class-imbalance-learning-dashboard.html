1. Start here — the big picture

Everything below is written for someone unfamiliar with machine learning. Numbers marked verified come straight from the repository artifacts; interpretation marks our explanatory reading of them.

What is BRFSS?

BRFSS (Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System) is a large, ongoing telephone health survey run by the US CDC. Each year it interviews hundreds of thousands of American adults about their health — exercise, smoking, diet, chronic conditions, and more. Our project uses the 2015–2024 surveys: 2,297,168 respondents in total. The question we ask of the data: can we predict whether a person has diabetes from their survey answers?

The target variable is DIABETE4 (“have you ever been told you have diabetes?”). Only 17.06% of respondents answer “Yes”. The models use 15 predictors (age group, BMI category, general health, smoking, income, etc. — all integer-coded survey answers). YEAR is metadata only, never a predictor.

Source: P4_Integration_Evaluation/report/Person_4_Integration_Report.md §2 · P4_Integration_Evaluation/results/input_validation.json

What is “class imbalance” — and why is accuracy misleading?

Imagine 100 survey respondents. About 83 do not have diabetes, 17 do:

A lazy model that always predicts “No diabetes” would be right 83 times out of 100 — 83% accuracy — while detecting zero actual diabetes cases. That is the accuracy trap: when one class dominates, accuracy rewards doing nothing useful.

This is exactly what happened in our baseline (no balancing). With XGBoost it reached 0.835 accuracy — the highest accuracy in the whole study — but its recall was only 0.123: it missed 68,711 of the 78,366 diabetic patients in the test set (about 7 in 8). verified

Source: P4_Integration_Evaluation/results/combined_results.csv — row No Balancing / XGBoost (Accuracy 0.8354, Recall 0.1232, FN 68,711 of 78,366 positives)

The base paper: Divide and Recombine (D&R)

Our starting point is the paper “Divide and Recombine Approaches for Fitting Logistic Regression to Large-Scale Health Surveillance Data: Application to Diabetes Risk Prediction in BRFSS” (Nayem & Biswas, Scientific Reports). Its core idea:

  1. Divide — split the massive dataset into smaller, manageable subsets.
  2. Fit — run logistic regression independently on each subset (no communication needed between them).
  3. Recombine — combine the subset estimates into one final model that approximates the full-data result.

The paper’s headline result: on BRFSS-scale data D&R matched centralized estimation (>99.8% relative efficiency) while cutting computation time by >52% and memory use by 77–89% (validated with Monte Carlo simulations of 5M synthetic observations × 1,000 replications, plus BRFSS 2014–2024 with 2.4M+ rows).

How our P1–P4 work relates to the base paper — and how it differs.
Shared context: same BRFSS diabetes-prediction setting, same large-survey-data challenges, logistic regression as a core classifier.
Different research question: the base paper’s primary focus is computational efficiency — how to fit models faster on huge data. Our P1–P4 study asks a modelling question instead: given we can already fit the models, how should we handle the 17%-vs-83% class imbalance so the model actually detects diabetes cases? Our contribution is the systematic comparison of imbalance-handling techniques, not faster fitting. interpretation
Base-paper facts: abstract of Nayem & Biswas (Scientific Reports, doi:10.1038/s41598-026-46927-7) · D&R foundations: Guha et al. (2009, 2012); Cleveland & Hafen (2014)

4. Metrics — what each number means

All metrics are computed on the same frozen test set (459,434 people: 381,068 without diabetes, 78,366 with diabetes) at a fixed 0.5 decision threshold.

Accuracy Overall correctness

Of 100 people, how many did the model label correctly? Misleading here: predicting “No” for everyone scores 0.83 without finding a single case.

Precision Trust in a “Yes”

When the model says “diabetes”, how often is it right? Falls when we push the model to flag more cases (more false alarms).

Recall Cases caught

Of 100 truly diabetic people, how many did the model find? The most clinically important metric for screening — missing a case can mean missed care.

F1 Balance of the two

Single number balancing precision and recall for the diabetic class. Our headline metric: baseline 0.15–0.22 → best 0.445.

ROC-AUC Ranking quality

Does the model rank a randomly chosen diabetic patient above a non-diabetic one? Ignores the 0.5 threshold — barely moved by resampling (0.77–0.78 throughout).

PR-AUC Ranking quality (rare class)

Like ROC-AUC but focused on the minority class. Also threshold-free, also nearly unchanged by resampling — the key clue that resampling shifts the operating point, not the ranking.

5. Findings — which approach wins, and under which metric

The central lesson: there is no single “best” technique — the winner depends on what you want the model to do. Each finding below states its source, meaning, conclusion, and limitation.

Finding 1. Plain Random Oversampling is the F1 champion verified

Value & source: F1 = 0.4435 (Logistic Regression), 0.4441 (Random Forest), 0.4453 (XGBoost) — the top F1 for every classifier. Source: combined_results.csv, rows Random Oversampling (P1).

What it means: the simplest method — duplicating existing diabetic training rows until classes balance — beats all eleven fancier alternatives on the balanced precision/recall measure.

Conclusion: on this dataset the marginal value of sophisticated synthesis over naive duplication is near zero for F1; with 313,462 real minority examples, simple duplication balances the loss without visible overfitting. interpretation

Limitation: the margin over SMOTE (0.4415) is tiny; with n = 459,434 even tiny gaps are statistically significant — the practical gap is small.

Finding 2. ADASYN catches the most cases — at a price verified

Value & source: Recall = 0.7716 (ADASYN + Random Forest), the highest recall in the study; ADASYN + XGBoost 0.7689. Source: combined_results.csv, rows ADASYN (P1).

What it means: ADASYN focuses synthetic data on “hard” minority examples, pushing the model to say “Yes” most aggressively — catching 77% of diabetic cases.

Conclusion: if the use-case is screening where missing a case is costly and false alarms are tolerable, ADASYN’s operating point is the one to choose — but precision drops to ~0.30 and accuracy to ~0.65. Higher recall ≠ best overall model. interpretation

Limitation: ~2 in 3 “diabetes” flags are false alarms; downstream triage capacity must absorb that.

Finding 3. ENN: the best accuracy-preserving option verified

Value & source: ENN + XGBoost: F1 0.4420 (runner-up overall) with accuracy 0.7935 and ROC-AUC 0.7799 — vs ROS accuracy of only 0.6829. Source: combined_results.csv, row ENN / XGBoost (P3).

What it means: instead of inventing synthetic patients, ENN deletes 470,885 ambiguous majority rows the 3-nearest-neighbours rule disagrees with — cleaning the boundary rather than fabricating data.

Conclusion: when accuracy/precision must be preserved (e.g. triage with limited follow-up), ENN is the best choice: near-champion F1 without the oversamplers’ accuracy collapse. interpretation

Limitation: still discards real data; cleaning aggressiveness is a judgement call, not a law of nature.

Finding 4. Tomek Links ≈ doing (almost) nothing — and that’s informative verified

Value & source: Tomek Links F1 = 0.2242 / 0.1636 / 0.2140 — essentially baseline level (0.2166 / 0.1531 / 0.2034) — yet it holds the highest ROC-AUC in the study (0.7803, XGBoost). McNemar: statistically indistinguishable from baseline on all three classifiers. Source: combined_results.csv + statistical_tests_mcnemar.csv.

What it means: removing just 7,161 borderline majority rows (0.47% of them) changes almost nothing — proof that gentle cleaning preserves the baseline’s excellent ranking quality.

Conclusion: if you want the baseline’s calibrated probabilities and ranking untouched, Tomek Links is the shallowest possible intervention. interpretation

Limitation: it does not solve the recall problem at all (recall stays ~0.09–0.14).

Finding 5. The unifying explanation: resampling moves the operating point, not the ranking verified

Value & source: XGBoost PR-AUC: ROS 0.4176 vs baseline 0.4176 — identical; ROC-AUC 0.7802 vs 0.7802. The ROC/PR curves of family champions nearly coincide (figures/05_family_champion_curves.png).

What it means: balancing the training set teaches the classifier that diabetes prevalence is ~50% instead of 17%, so at the fixed 0.5 threshold it says “Yes” far more often. The model’s ability to rank patients by risk barely changes — only the decision behaviour at one threshold does.

Conclusion: for this research, technique choice is effectively a choice of operating point (plus a modest ENN cleaning dividend) — not a modelling upgrade. A threshold-tuned baseline might close much of the gap: flagged as future work. interpretation

Limitation: threshold 0.5 was fixed by team protocol; the threshold-moving comparison was not run.

What we must disclose — limitations & caveats verified

  • † Subsample techniques: SVM-SMOTE and SMOTE-N were trained on a shared stratified 50,000-row subsample (41,472 No / 8,528 Yes), not the full 1.84M training set — indicative, not strictly like-for-like.
  • KMeans-SMOTE non-default parameters (kmeans_estimator=100, cluster_balance_threshold=0.2) — library defaults failed on the all-categorical feature space; documented in P2’s protocol.
  • SVM-SMOTE extrapolation: 12,314 synthetic values (2.49%) fall one code below the observed per-feature minimum — inherent to out_step=0.5, reported in P2’s audit.
  • XGBoost cross-machine variability: re-running on different hardware changes XGBoost metrics by ~0.002 (1,238 label flips) — XGBoost hist is not bit-reproducible across thread counts. Differences below ~0.002 are machine noise, not method differences. LR/RF are bit-identical.
  • Significance ≠ importance: with n = 459,434, even trivially small differences become “significant” — that is why every McNemar p-value is reported with effect sizes (accuracy/recall differences), and why the report stresses practical gaps over p-values.
  • Single split, fixed threshold: one 80/20 split (no cross-validation); decision threshold fixed at 0.5 by protocol — a threshold-tuned baseline could narrow the resampled models’ advantage (future work).
  • No external cohort; patients may repeat across survey years; no calibration analysis (reliability diagrams/Brier score).
Sources: P4_Integration_Evaluation/report/Person_4_Integration_Report.md §4 & §9 · P2_Advanced_Oversampling/results/validation_report.json (out-of-range audit) · P2_Advanced_Oversampling/PROTOCOL_P2.md
What is McNemar’s test, in plain English?

To ask “do two techniques really behave differently?”, P4 compared their predictions patient-by-patient on the same 459,434 test rows. McNemar’s test looks only at the patients where the two models disagree (A right / B wrong vs A wrong / B right) and asks whether the disagreement is lopsided enough to rule out chance. Holm correction adjusts for running 198 such tests so we don’t get fooled by multiple comparisons. Result: 191/198 pairs differ significantly — and the 7 that don’t (e.g. Tomek Links vs baseline) tell us which techniques are effectively equivalent.

8. What should our supervisor take away from this research?

  1. The study is complete and validated: 12 techniques × 3 classifiers on one shared BRFSS 2015–2024 protocol, with machine-checked gates (82/82 input checks, 36/36 P4 checks) and paired statistical testing (191/198 McNemar significant after Holm).
  2. Class imbalance handling roughly doubles minority-class F1 (0.15–0.22 → up to 0.445) by trading accuracy/precision for recall — the baseline’s 0.835 accuracy masked a recall of only 0.09–0.14.
  3. Simplicity wins: plain Random Oversampling is the F1 champion on all three classifiers; sophisticated oversamplers add essentially nothing on F1 (statistically indistinguishable label behaviour for ROS ≈ SMOTE ≈ SMOTE-Tomek).
  4. The scientifically interesting result is the mechanism: resampling shifts the operating point (threshold behaviour), not the ranking quality — PR-AUC for XGBoost is identical (0.4176) with and without balancing. Technique choice is therefore a decision about the desired recall/precision mix, not a modelling upgrade.
  5. ENN is the accuracy-preserving alternative (F1 0.442, accuracy 0.794 vs 0.683 for ROS) — the most valuable non-oversampling intervention is cleaning, not synthesis.
  6. Limitations are documented, not hidden: † subsample techniques, KMeans-SMOTE non-defaults, SVM-SMOTE extrapolation (2.49%), XGBoost ~0.002 machine noise, single split, fixed 0.5 threshold, no external cohort.
  7. Recommended next steps: threshold-tuning comparison against the baseline (the cheapest experiment, using saved predictions); cross-validation of the leading techniques; calibration analysis; and an external validation cohort before any clinical framing.
